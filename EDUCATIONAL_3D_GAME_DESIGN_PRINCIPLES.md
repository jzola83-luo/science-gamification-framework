# Educational 3D Game Design Principles

**Version:** 1.0  
**Evidence base:** Lim, Nonis, & Hedberg (2006), an exploratory study of Quest Atlantis in Primary Four science lessons.  
**Scope:** A reusable starting point for designing and reviewing 3D games intended to support learning, especially science learning.

This guide is grounded in one small exploratory study, not a universal recipe. The study involved eight students in one school, so its findings are useful design evidence but do not establish that every principle will work for every age, subject, or game. Treat the principles below as research-informed questions to test with learners.

## How to read the evidence labels

- **Study finding** means Lim et al. reported or observed this in their study.
- **Design synthesis** means a practical recommendation inferred from those findings. It is not a tested result from the paper.
- When adding research, record the source, population, context, method, and limitations. Keep evidence distinct from interpretation.

## Principles

### P1. Make learning actions part of gameplay

**Design synthesis.** The actions needed to advance should exercise the knowledge or thinking the game is meant to teach. A compelling 3D world can attract attention without ensuring that learners engage with the learning task.

**Study finding.** Students sometimes explored the virtual world while neglecting quests, submitting incomplete work, or losing their way. The authors explicitly distinguish engagement in the 3D space from engagement in the task.

**Check:** Can a learner make progress by investigating, reasoning, explaining, or applying the target idea? Is there an attractive activity that lets them bypass the learning goal?

### P2. Build quests around inquiry and meaningful problems

**Design synthesis.** Give learners a question or problem, opportunities to investigate, and a reason to interpret and communicate what they find. Connect virtual activity with physical experiments or real-world contexts when that supports the learning goal.

**Study finding.** QA quests could involve gathering information, conducting experiments or interviews, interpreting and analysing evidence, and reflecting. Students' post-assessment answers showed improvement in explaining water-cycle concepts, though the study design cannot isolate the game's causal effect.

**Check:** Does each quest ask learners to do more than retrieve facts? Do they use evidence to explain a result or make a decision?

### P3. Match the experience to learner readiness

**Design synthesis.** Check learners' prior knowledge, reading level, language needs, computer skills, and experience with reflection. Teach unfamiliar controls and ways of working before they become barriers to the subject matter.

**Study finding.** Students struggled with the language in quests, computer operations such as screenshots and copy/paste, and the unfamiliar task of reflecting on learning. These difficulties contributed to loss of task orientation and frustration.

**Check:** Have learners tried the controls and a sample task? Are instructions understandable to the intended audience? Is prerequisite vocabulary explained?

### P4. Scaffold autonomy, then fade support

**Design synthesis.** Provide clear goals, models, guiding questions, prompts, examples, and checklists. Let learners make meaningful choices while offering timely help when they become stuck.

**Study finding.** Templates, guiding questions, links, and keywords were intended to reduce the cognitive demands of open-ended learning. Orientation activities and teacher demonstrations helped students navigate tasks and supported engagement.

**Check:** Does a learner know what to do next and how to tell whether the work is complete? Can support be reduced as competence grows?

### P5. Keep navigation and interface demands manageable

**Design synthesis.** Make objectives, quest locations, maps, controls, and task progress easy to find. Use 3D where it contributes to understanding or meaningful action, and avoid navigation work that competes with learning for attention.

**Study finding.** Some learners had difficulty locating quests and became distracted by free exploration. The paper notes that effort spent navigating can reduce the mental resources available for the task.

**Check:** Can learners reach the learning activity without wandering or repeatedly asking where to go? Does each important 3D interaction serve a learning or play purpose?

### P6. Teach reflection and make thinking visible

**Design synthesis.** Treat reflection as a skill to learn. Model concise prompts about predictions, observations, evidence, explanations, and changes in understanding. Give learners time to respond and use their explanations to guide feedback.

**Study finding.** Students found QA's reflection questions difficult and unfamiliar; several expressed helplessness. The teacher had to guide pairs through the reflection section.

**Check:** Have learners seen examples of a useful reflection? Does the game provide enough time and structure to explain what they learned?

### P7. Tie story, feedback, and rewards to learning progress

**Design synthesis.** Use story and rewards to give effort a meaningful context. Feedback should show what the learner understands and what to try next; reward signals should reinforce progress on the learning goals.

**Study finding.** Students were excited by QA's 3D setting, avatar control, storyline, points, and rewards. These features created initial interest, but immersion could also distract from the quests.

**Check:** Do rewards reflect useful learning behaviours or progress? Does feedback help learners improve, rather than only signal completion or rank?

### P8. Design collaboration as a learning activity

**Design synthesis.** Give pairs or groups a shared problem and reasons to compare evidence, discuss explanations, and combine contributions. Chat or multiplayer presence alone does not guarantee collaboration.

**Study finding.** QA provided synchronous and asynchronous communication that could support shared knowledge construction. Whether these opportunities were taken up depended on how the game was situated in lessons.

**Check:** Does each learner have a meaningful contribution? Is there a shared outcome that requires discussion or coordination?

### P9. Support the teacher and the classroom context

**Design synthesis.** Make it practical for teachers to orient learners, see progress, identify stuck students, give feedback, and fit the activity into curriculum time. Plan for debriefing and assessment as well as play.

**Study finding.** Teacher orientation and intervention supported engagement. Curriculum time and assessment expectations were major obstacles: the water-cycle work took about three hours in QA, while the teacher reported teaching it in about one hour by textbook methods.

**Check:** Can a teacher tell who needs help and why? Does the activity fit a realistic lesson plan, including setup, play, discussion, and reflection?

### P10. Measure learning separately from enjoyment

**Design synthesis.** Evaluate enjoyment, engagement with the 3D environment, engagement with learning tasks, quality of learner work, and conceptual understanding as distinct outcomes. Use more than one source of evidence where possible.

**Study finding.** The study combined observations, interviews, submitted work, and pre/post assessments. Students' post-test scores improved, while observed engagement levels remained modest. This illustrates why a single engagement or satisfaction measure is insufficient.

**Check:** What would count as evidence that learners understood the target concept? Are measures of that outcome distinct from time spent playing or self-reported enjoyment?

## Project review checklist

Before committing to a design, answer these questions:

1. What specific learning goal does the game support, and what in-game action exercises it?
2. What evidence will learners gather, interpret, create, or explain?
3. What prior knowledge, language, device skills, and reflection skills does the game assume?
4. Where might learners get lost, distracted, cognitively overloaded, or stuck?
5. What scaffolds, feedback, and teacher tools help them recover and progress?
6. How do story, rewards, and collaboration serve the learning activity?
7. How will learning be assessed separately from game engagement?
8. Does the complete activity fit available lesson time and curriculum expectations?
9. What learner test or classroom observation will challenge the design assumptions?
